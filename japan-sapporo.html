Sapporo: Late-Season Sakura, Hokkaido Ice Cream & a Day with Bears

Day 1 (29 Apr): Sakura, Hill of the Buddha & Pole Town

We took an early morning flight from Tokyo to Sapporo, then the Airport Limousine bus into the city. Hokkaido is its own country inside Japan — colder, slower, with mountains hanging in the distance and the air doing that crisp thing that makes you want to wear a sweater even in late April. And yet — sakura. We had timed it just right. Hokkaido blooms weeks after Tokyo, so the cherry blossoms we'd already said goodbye to were back, this time against a chillier sky.

We checked into the Park Hotel — 5-star, comfortable, but with the very Japanese rationing of complimentary water (one small bottle per night, please budget accordingly). After dropping bags, we headed out. Our first stop was the Hill of the Buddha, around an hour and a half from the hotel. Designed by Tadao Ando, it's one of those places photos do not prepare you for. You approach through a tunnel and the giant Buddha statue rises slowly into view, framed by hundreds of thousands of lavender plants — not in bloom yet in April, but you could feel exactly what it would look like in summer.

Back in central Sapporo, we walked the Pole Town underground shopping arcade — a kilometre of shops, cafés and warmth, the very welcome alternative to a chilly street. Hokkaido is famous for dairy and they earn it. The ice cream here is genuinely different — creamier, milkier, the kind of thing you eat slowly because you know it's a one-off. We tried two flavours, then went back for a third.

Day 2 (30 Apr): Noboribetsu, Bears & Lake Toya

Day two we'd booked a full-day organised tour. We normally plan our own routes, but Hokkaido's countryside is spread out and the tour was cheaper than renting a car. The guide explained the history of every stop in good English — and we could just sit, look out the window, and zone out between attractions. Sometimes that's exactly what you need.

First stop: Noboribetsu Hell Valley. Volcanic terrain, sulphur in the air, steam rising from cracks in red-orange rock. It looks like a film set — the name "Hell Valley" is, for once, completely earned. From there we drove to the Bear Ranch and Mount Usu Ropeway. The bears, watching humans throw apples down to them, looked entirely unimpressed. The ropeway pulled us up to a viewing platform with the caldera laid out below — green crater lake on one side, the Pacific on the other.

Then Lake Toya. We stopped at a local farm for fresh yogurt and — yes — more ice cream. (At this point we accepted that Hokkaido is a place you ice-cream your way through.) The lake itself is huge and calm, with mountains on every side. We rolled back into Sapporo around 4pm, with just enough energy left to walk through Odori Park, climb up the Sapporo TV Tower for a city view, and call it a night.

Hokkaido is the version of Japan no one warns you about — quieter, colder, bigger, and somehow more generous. Two days were not enough. Two weeks would be a good start.

A Note on Japanese Customer Service

One small story worth saving. The first tour we'd booked got cancelled — and the tour company refunded us in cash, in person, with an actual apology. No emails, no "5–7 business days." That kind of professionalism stops being remarkable in Japan only because it happens everywhere, all the time. Hokkaido was no exception.

Must-Do in Sapporo

  • Hill of the Buddha: The tunnel reveal is unforgettable.
  • Hokkaido Ice Cream: Anywhere. Everywhere. Trust us.
  • Pole Town Arcade: A warm underground escape.
  • Noboribetsu Hell Valley: Volcanic, otherworldly, smells like sulphur.
  • Mount Usu Ropeway: Crater lake on one side, ocean on the other.
  • Bear Ranch: Toss them apples. They've seen everything.
  • Lake Toya & Local Farms: Yogurt, dairy, calm water.
  • Sapporo TV Tower & Odori Park: A simple end-of-day city view.
